Why the costs sit where they do

You both generate the leads together and split 50/50. So every dollar in and every dollar out belongs to the partnership, no matter whose account it passed through. Whose card was charged is custody, not ownership. That is also exactly why the $500 changes no profit figure — it never left the partnership, it only changed pocket.

Never type a payment between you into Spent or TFN Cost The $500 belongs in the Berry To Tyson column and nowhere else. If it were ever logged as a cost, it would invent $500 of expense that never happened, drop the reported profit by $500 and move each partner's share by $250 — and the sheet would never complain. A transfer between partners is not a business cost.

The one control that would actually catch bad data Add two columns you fill in from outside: money actually banked (from the bank or the client statement) and the ad invoice. Two traps to know about 1. Never move money between Spent and TFN Cost. If Berry reimburses Tyson $100 of ad spend and someone records it as E2−100 and J2+100, the total cost rises $33 — because the 0.67 applies to E2 and not to J2. Each partner's share moves $16.50, the payment moves $83.50, and both checks still read 0. Money moving between partners is not a cost — it does not belong in either column. 2. Never SUM the check column on a totals row. A +$50 error on one row and a −$50 error on another sum to a clean zero. Use =COUNTIF(U2:U1000,"<>0") instead — it must read 0.
How much any error costs you — one rule An error in the shared pool (a wrong lead count, a wrong spend) is split between you, so it moves the payment by half the mistake. An error in who held the money (a cost logged against the wrong partner, revenue credited to the wrong one) moves the payment by the full amount. That is why the columns saying who paid and who collected matter twice as much as the totals.

What is proved, and how

Six properties, each proved to hold for all inputs and then tested on 4,000 randomised cases with exact fractions — profits, losses, zero rows, and two, three and five partners:

#PropertyWhy it matters to you
1The settlements always sum to exactly 0Money is never created or lost in the split
2After settling, each partner sits exactly on his shareThe transfer does what it's meant to do
3The two positions always sum to the netThe books close — what you each hold accounts for the whole result
4That settlement is the only one that worksThere is no rival "fairer" number to argue about
5Additivity — daily settlements sum to the monthly oneSettle per day, per week or per month; identical answer
6Invariance — moving a cost between partners changes neither the net nor either shareTagging the wrong payer cannot corrupt your profit figure — only the transfer

Then the spreadsheet formulas themselves were run against that model on 3,000 more random rows — different lead counts, spends, rates and TFN values — and matched exactly every time, on profitable days as well as losing ones. That is the step that catches a formula which is accidentally right at today's numbers and wrong everywhere else.

Two honest limits on that list #3 is weaker than it sounds. It says your two positions net to the result — it does not prove each dollar was tagged to the right person. A cost counted against both of you and a cost counted against neither cancel each other exactly. That is why the section above exists.

#5 needs the split to stay the same. Additivity holds while both rows use 50/50. If one day were ever split differently, day-by-day settlements and a settle-once-at-month-end would no longer agree, and there would be no single right answer. Keep the ratio constant, or settle each period separately.
