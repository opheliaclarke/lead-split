The only rule you need
Your settlement = half the net  −  what you collected  +  what you paid
Same rule for both partners. Positive means you receive; negative means you pay.

The bottom row is the whole integrity test. The three components add to the net, to the total revenue and to the total cost respectively — and the settlements add to exactly zero, because one partner pays what the other receives and nothing evaporates.

Why the costs sit where they do

You both generate the leads together and split 50/50. So every dollar in and every dollar out belongs to the partnership, no matter whose account it passed through. Whose card was charged is custody, not ownership.

What the two check cells do

These are the reason this can be a permanent basis. If either one is not 0, stop and look — something is genuinely wrong. And the number it shows is the size of the mistake.

So: the checks are a complete test that every dollar is attributed to exactly one partner. They are not a test of the split ratio, of your typing, or of which direction you actually send the money. For those, read the two Share columns and obey the sign on Settle.

Why ROUND(…,6) and not a bare sum Sheets stores 1193 × 0.67 as 799.3100000000001. Left raw, these check cells would show things like 3.6e-12 on roughly half of all rows and look broken. (Today's row cancels to a clean zero by luck, not design.) The worst residue measured across 100,000 random rows was 7.3e-12 — a real error is a billion times bigger, so rounding to 6 decimals hides the noise and nothing else.

When Berry's other costs arrive

You said the $30 TFN is all of Berry's cost for now and the rest comes later. When it does, put it in a new column — say V2 — and change exactly two cells:

L2 — add the new cost to the pool
=I2+J2+V2

Both, always. A cost must enter the pool and be attributed to whoever paid it. If you update L2 and forget Q2, check cell T2 immediately shows the exact amount you forgot. Same pattern for a new Tyson cost: add it to L2 and subtract it in P2.

The verdict on the business, separately

The split was never the problem.

Those 25 leads cost $33.17 each to produce and sold for $8. You lose $25.17 on every lead you sell, and revenue covered 24% of cost.

To break even on that $1,193 spend at $8 a lead you needed 104 leads. You got 25. Fixing the formula tells you the loss honestly; it does not make it smaller. The lever is cost-per-lead or price-per-lead, not the split.
